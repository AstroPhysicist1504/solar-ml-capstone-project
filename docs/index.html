The approach. Building load and solar (PV) generation are forecast by separate pipelines — each benchmarked across three models (ELM, XGBoost, MLP) at three horizons (1, 6, and 24 hours ahead), against naive and seasonal baselines, using a chronological train/test split with equal-effort hyperparameter tuning applied to every model.

The finding that shaped the EMS design. The reference building's load (roughly 16–28 kW through the day) is several times larger than the modeled 5 kW system's peak output (~4 kW). Rather than force a full supply/demand balance that would trivially show "deficit" at every hour, the EMS layer instead reports what share of demand solar actually covers — an honest, more informative framing given the real scale relationship.

Load forecasting

Boys' hostel building (I-BLEND, IIIT Delhi), 2013–2017. Target: next-hour, 6-hour, and 24-hour ahead power draw.

RMSE by model and horizon (lower is better)

ELM is the most consistent performer, beating the strongest available baseline at every horizon by a stable ~6.7–6.8% margin regardless of how far ahead it's forecasting.

6-hour-ahead is the hardest horizon for every model — a "same hour yesterday" seasonal baseline is unusually strong there, and only ELM manages to beat it.

MLP shows one instructive anomaly: its 1-hour result regressed after hyperparameter tuning, traced to a single validation split not generalizing to the test period — reported as-is rather than re-tuned away.

Solar PV forecasting

Delhi (NASA POWER, 2020–2024), converted to PV power via pvlib physical modeling for a nominal 5 kW system. Daylight-only evaluation.

RMSE by model and horizon, daylight hours only (lower is better)

All three models decisively beat every baseline at every horizon — a much cleaner result than load forecasting, because real-time weather features (cloud amount, clear-sky index) give the models information no naive time-based rule can access.

The advantage shrinks predictably with horizon: from ~65% better than the toughest baseline at 1 hour, to ~15% at 24 hours — current weather is highly informative about the very next hour, less so a full day out.

XGBoost and MLP are essentially tied on accuracy throughout; XGBoost was selected for the EMS pipeline for deployment reasons — no scaler artifacts to keep synchronized, bounded output behavior, and built-in feature importance.

EMS balance: solar coverage of load

Both models' 1-hour-ahead predictions combined by hour-of-day (IST) into a composite-day profile — the two datasets don't share real calendar dates, so this is a representative-day estimate, not a literal date-matched forecast.

Predicted load vs. predicted PV output, by hour of day

Solar coverage of load, by hour of day

Load and generation run almost in anti-phase. Load bottoms out at 15.9 kW around 6:00, right as the sun is only just rising, and peaks at 28.3 kW at 18:00 — right after the sun has set. Solar's own peak (~2.4 kW, around 10:00–11:00) lands in a period when load is comparatively low but still 7–8× larger than what the system can supply.

The result is a modest but real contribution: solar covers a meaningful 10–14% of load during late morning, tapering to effectively nothing outside daylight hours — consistent with a small rooftop system supplementing, not replacing, grid supply for a building of this size.

How this works. Both models' trained parameters were exported directly from the training run and run here as real computation — the ELM's random-projection matrices and output weights, and XGBoost's full 500-tree ensemble — executed in JavaScript, not pre-recorded per combination. The cloud-cover slider rewrites the weather-dependent inputs (GHI, DNI, diffuse irradiance, clear-sky index, cloud amount) proportionally from each scenario's real recorded values before both models re-run — so moving it explores a genuine hypothetical, not a different lookup entry. Predictions typically match the original Python output to within a small fraction of a percent; tiny residual differences come from XGBoost's internal use of 32-bit floating point in places versus 64-bit here.

Methodology notes

Decisions made to keep the model comparison fair and the results defensible.

  • Chronological train/test splits, no shuffling
    Every split respects time order — training always precedes testing, with a further chronological sub-split used for hyperparameter validation. This prevents the model from ever training on data that comes after what it's being tested on.
  • Equal-budget hyperparameter tuning across all three models
    Each model (ELM, XGBoost, MLP) receives the same tuning effort — four hyperparameter combinations, two knobs each — so no model is favored by receiving more attention than the others.
  • Benchmarked against the strongest baseline, not a convenient one
    Three naive baselines (plain persistence, 24-hour-seasonal, 168-hour-seasonal) are computed at every horizon, and models are judged against whichever one is actually toughest at that horizon — not a single fixed baseline that might flatter the result.
  • Daylight-only evaluation for solar
    Roughly half of all hours are night, where any model trivially predicts ~0. Evaluating across all 24 hours dilutes real forecasting error; daylight-only scoring (and daylight-only hyperparameter selection) reports the number that actually reflects forecasting skill.
  • A caught and corrected pipeline bug
    An early version of the clear-sky-index feature used NaN at night as a divide-by-zero guard — which silently dropped ~48% of the PV dataset from training and testing via a blanket dropna(). Identified via an implausible negative PV prediction, fixed by filling with 0, and all PV results re-generated on the corrected data.
